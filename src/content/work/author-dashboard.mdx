Most publishing platforms stop at distribution. They get a book onto the marketplaces and consider the job done.

But for the author, that's where the questions start. Where is the book selling? How much have I actually earned? Who's reading it? So I built **AuthorOS** — a workspace for a self-publishing company that connects distribution, sales, royalties, and reader engagement into a single view, and extends the author relationship long after launch day.

## The problem

The company distributes books across several publishing and marketplace channels. The distribution worked. The visibility didn't.

- Authors published, then went blind — no view of how the book was doing
- Sales data lived in a different place for every channel
- Royalties were reconciled by hand and shared on a schedule
- Distribution reach was invisible — authors didn't know where their book had actually landed
- The only way to get an answer was to email and wait for a report

The friction was structural. Every author question turned into a manual support request, and every report was a snapshot that was stale the moment it was sent.

## The system

One record per book. Everything an author needs to understand it — distribution, sales, royalties, engagement — hangs off that record and updates as the channels report back.

## Small decisions that matter

The difference between an admin panel and a product authors *want* to open lives in the details:

- **Author-first language.** No internal jargon from the publishing side — every label is written for the person who wrote the book.
- **A glance, then the detail.** The home view answers the three questions an author has first; everything else is one click deeper, never in the way.
- **Honest numbers.** When channel data is delayed, the workspace says so rather than showing a confident-but-wrong figure.
- **No empty states that feel broken.** A freshly published book with no sales yet gets a real "here's what to expect" view, not a blank dashboard.

## Building publishing infrastructure

**The product is the visibility, not the dashboard.** Anyone can render a chart. The value here was connecting fragmented channel data into one trustworthy picture of a book — that connection is the product, and the dashboard is just where you see it.

**Information architecture is the real design work.** The hard part wasn't styling screens; it was deciding what an author sees first, second, and third. Leading with the book and pushing detail one level down turned a pile of metrics into something an author could actually read.

**Closing a workflow gap beats adding a feature.** The opportunity wasn't a better writing tool — it was the silence after publication. Filling that gap, with a workspace that answers the author's own questions, is what turns distribution into a relationship.
